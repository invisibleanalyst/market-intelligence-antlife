When a larger market
isn’t the answer.

Before estimating the market for an offer, work through the economics of serving one customer.

In this observationStart with one customerWhen scale helpsA test for the pilot

A country with a growing student population looks promising on an ed-tech shortlist. Yet an enrolment figure tells a team little about the price a family will pay or what it costs to reach that family.

Start with one customer.

Take a simple example. A customer pays $120 over a year and costs $150 to acquire and serve. The company loses $30 on that customer before covering any fixed costs. At those prices, selling the same offer to 500 customers produces a $15,000 shortfall before entry costs.

When scale helps.

Scale can help when it changes a specific cost. A distribution partner might reach families more cheaply. A school contract could change both the price per student and the cost of delivery. Each possibility suggests a different test.

In the independent East African expansion study, the model projected a first-year loss in all four markets. Moving up the country ranking would have narrowed the projected loss. The acquisition and service costs still needed attention.

A test for the pilot.

Choose the assumption carrying the case. If a partner is central to the plan, a pilot could record how many paying customers that channel attracts, the cost of reaching them and the revenue each brings in.

Once those figures are available, the team can calculate the contribution from one customer and the sales volume it would take to recover the entry cost.

Source

The four-market comparison comes from the independent ed-tech expansion study. The $120 price and $150 cost above are example assumptions from the separate calculator.
