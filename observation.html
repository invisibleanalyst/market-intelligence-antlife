When a larger market
isn’t the answer.

A market can be attractive and expansion can still be the wrong next step.

In this observationThe unit comes firstWhat scale changesA more useful question

Market comparisons often begin with size: more potential customers, a growing population, a larger addressable market. Those are useful inputs. They do not settle the economics of serving a customer.

The unit comes first.

If the revenue from a customer is lower than the cost of acquiring and serving them, each additional customer adds to the deficit. A larger market offers more opportunities to repeat the same loss.

Expansion amplifies the economics you bring with you.

What scale changes.

Scale can change costs, prices and customer behaviour. Those changes need to be examined, not assumed. A model should show which improvement is required, how large it needs to be, and what evidence supports it.

In the supplied four-market ed-tech example, the historical model showed negative first-year results across every candidate market. Its useful contribution was to redirect attention from a country ranking to the underlying assumptions.

A more useful question.

Before asking “which market is largest?”, ask “what has to be true for the next customer to make a positive contribution?” Then ask how many customers are needed to cover the fixed cost of entry.

Those questions do not make the decision automatic. They make the reasoning easier to examine.

Context and limits

This editorial observation draws on the analyst’s supplied historical expansion exhibit. It is not a new empirical research finding, and does not claim that the modelled losses occurred.
